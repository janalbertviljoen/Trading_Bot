# Starting investor profile: the founder's portfolio split

A reference for investors at the start of their investing life, with a long time horizon
and therefore room for more risk. It shows how the founder of Assay builds a
diversified core and adds a small satellite of Midas picks. It is a worked example for starting
investors, labelled illustrative: one person's approach, not advice or a recommended
allocation, and the weights below are a guideline, not the founder's actual balances or
weightings.

For general questions about how to invest, how much to hold in shares and bonds, or how
the mix should change with age, the reference is Benjamin Graham, *The Intelligent
Investor*, and Midas's free chat sticks to Graham for these questions. Midas's own
stance: "I only read the book; he wrote it." When choosing companies, Midas follows the
method Berkshire Hathaway describes in its published letters to shareholders. Midas
quotes both from their published work, cites the letters by year, never speaks as
either of them, and implies no endorsement by Graham, Berkshire or Warren Buffett.

## Full disclosure: the founder's whole portfolio

The index split below is the founder's listed portfolio, not all of his wealth. Most of
it is property, through his own asset-management business, which owns and manages
residential property and short-term rental bookings in Cape Town. Property came first
because a house cannot be bought in small regular amounts: it takes a deposit and a home
loan, all at once. The business is set up as a company for its tax benefits, and its
returns are directed into the index split over time, until property is a small part of
the whole.

The lesson for a starting investor is the order, not the property: regular averaging into
a worldwide index split is the part anyone can begin with a small amount. A portfolio
concentrated in one city and one kind of asset, as the founder's is today, carries risks
of its own (local prices, rental rules, vacancies, and money that cannot be sold quickly),
which is why the plan is to dilute it. How a company structure is taxed depends on the
investor's circumstances; it is a question for a tax adviser, not for Midas.

## Who this profile is for

- Investors starting out, with decades before they need the money.
- Willing to hold mostly shares and ride out falls, because time lets them recover.
- Looking for a simple rule they can follow every month, not a forecast.

An investor nearer retirement, or one who cannot sit through a large fall, belongs closer
to Graham's defensive investor (see "As the investor grows older").

## Principles

1. **Diversify across companies worldwide.** The core holds index funds that together
   cover companies across the world: the United States, developed markets, Europe, the
   United Kingdom, Japan and emerging markets, with some property and bonds.
2. **Tax-free first.** Contributions go to the tax-free savings account first, up to its
   limit; after that, regular accounts are filled with the same weightings.
3. **Regular averaging.** A fixed amount is invested at regular intervals (every two
   weeks), so more units are bought when prices are low and fewer when they are high, and
   large sums are phased in over several months rather than at once.
4. **Buy and hold.** Holdings are not sold to time the market; the founder has never sold
   a share. Weightings are kept on course by directing new money to the parts that have
   fallen behind, not by selling.
5. **A small satellite.** About 5 to 10% of new investments goes to higher-risk
   opportunities researched with Midas. The core stays the foundation.

By region and kind: United States 35%, global and developed 20%, Europe and the United
Kingdom 15%, Asia and emerging markets 17%, property 7%, bonds 6%. Gold, through
Krugerrands, is an optional addition of up to about 7% in regular accounts.

Overlap is deliberate but worth watching: the Nasdaq 100, the S&P 500 and the
technology fund share their largest companies, so technology carries more weight than any
one line suggests. The Midas portfolio split shows this exposure by sector, country and
company, and shows what a satellite pick adds to it.

## Compared with Graham

Graham's defensive investor keeps between 25% and 75% in shares, with an even split as the
standard, and the rest in high-grade bonds (*The Intelligent Investor*, chapter 4). This
profile holds about 87% in shares, 7% in listed property and 6% in bonds, well beyond
Graham's limit. That is a deliberate choice for a long horizon, not Graham's advice, and
an investor should know it is more aggressive than the book; the path below brings it
inside Graham's range from 30 years before retirement.

## As the investor grows older

The mix moves from higher-risk assets (shares) to lower-risk assets (bonds and property)
as retirement approaches:

| Years to retirement | Higher risk (shares) | Lower risk (bonds and property) |
|---|---|---|
| 40 | 90% | 10% |
| 30 | 75% | 25% |
| 20 | 50% | 50% |
| 10 | 25% | 75% |

Between these points the mix moves gradually, mostly by directing new money to the side
that is below its target rather than by selling. The guideline split above, with 87% in
shares and 13% in property and bonds, is the 40-year end of this path.

The 40-year end matches Warren Buffett's own instruction for his wife's inheritance: 90%
in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund and 10% in short-term government bonds (letter to
Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, 2013).

From 30 years to retirement onwards the path stays inside Graham's range: 75% in shares is
his upper limit, the even split at 20 years is his standard, and 25% at 10 years is his
lower limit. Only the early, 40-year end goes beyond the book.

Listed property counts as lower risk here, as in the founder's own split. It pays a steady
income, but its prices can fall as sharply as shares in a crisis, and Graham's defensive
investor holds high-grade bonds, not property, on the safe side.
